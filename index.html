    §03Research · Transmission
    
      
    How does force actually travel through a financial system?
    
    
    
    
    
      
    A central bank changes one number. Somewhere a mortgage moves, a bank’s profits move, a currency moves — but not everywhere, and never by the same amount. I’ve come at that question from three distances: a bank, an economy, and a single tax. The answer is always in the friction.
    
    
    

    
    
      
    Fig. 02 — Three lenses on transmissionEach is a real piece of my work. Choose one.
    
      
    
        
        
        
      
    

      
    
        
    
          Paper · under peer review
          
    “To What Extent Did Federal Reserve Interest Rates Influence JPMorgan’s Financial Performance, 2018–2024?”
    
          
    4,500-word quantitative study across 28 quarters and three monetary regimes, built on FRED data and JPMorgan investor reports; constructs an original Bank Financial Performance Index.
    
        
    
        
    
          zk,t* = dk · Xk,t − Mk1.4826 · MADk
          BFPIt = 15 Σk=1…5 zk,t*
        
    
        
    
          
    My Bank Financial Performance Index standardises five indicators — earnings, capital, asset quality, liquidity, securities losses — against JPMorgan’s own history using a robust z-score built from the median and the median absolute deviation, so no single quarter can distort it. A direction coefficient dk flips indicators where higher means worse; the index is their equal-weighted mean, read in standard-deviation units around a benchmark of zero.
    
          
    Xk,t — reported value, indicator k, quarter t · Mk — median · MADk — median absolute deviation · 1.4826 — MAD→σ scaling · dk — +1 earnings / capital / liquidity, −1 asset-quality & securities

    The central paradox of the paper: raise the rate and profitability climbs with it (ρ = +0.722) — but the same hikes mark down the securities book, so the full index barely responds (ρ = +0.111). The lever’s power depends entirely on what you choose to measure.
    
        
    Between 0.23% and 3.95% — the paper’s regime means (Table 3) — values interpolate the reported figures. Beyond them, toward the 0.05% floor and the 5.33% peak the paper covers, readings extend each regime’s trend and are flagged as extrapolated. Associational, not causal — as the paper itself insists.
    
      
    

      
    
        
    
          Extended Essay
          
    “To What Extent Did Singapore’s Resilience & Solidarity Budgets Facilitate Economic Activity, 2020–2024?”
    
          
    Keynesian analysis of cash transfers and the Jobs Support Scheme; estimates an empirical fiscal multiplier of ≈ 0.88, evaluated against my own primary survey.
    
        
    
        
    
          k = ΔYΔG
          k = 11 − c(1−t) + m
        
    
        
    The fiscal multiplier asks how much national income (ΔY) a dollar of government spending (ΔG) generates. In a closed economy it exceeds one. In a small, trade-exposed economy every round of spending leaks abroad through imports (m) — and the multiplier can fall below one.
    
        
    
          
    
            
            
            
    My extended essay estimated Singapore’s multiplier at ≈ 0.88 — a dollar of stimulus returned about 88 cents of activity. The rest leaked out before it could multiply.

    Illustrative textbook form. The 0.88 is the essay’s empirical estimate.
    
      
    

      
    
        
    
          Internal Assessment
          
    “The United Kingdom’s Levy on Sports Gambling.”
    
          
    Microeconomic analysis of an indirect tax on a demerit good — demand-and-supply and welfare diagrams exposing the tension between correcting a market failure and raising revenue.
    
        
    
        
    A tax on a demerit good is meant to do two things at once: raise revenue and cut consumption. When demand is inelastic, the two aims pull apart — the same insensitivity that makes the tax an efficient earner makes it a poor deterrent.

    Gambling demand is inelastic — the levy fills the treasury while barely denting the behaviour it targets. That tension was the whole argument of the IA.

    §12Personal archive · Vol. 002 — Music · The Frequency Room
    
      
    Pink Floyd first. Then everyone else.
    
    
    
    
    Pink Floyd is the band I write about. The Rolling Stones, Oasis and Sade are the rest of the rotation — the records that are on when the economics is not. Below, a 1970s studio drawn as one machine: click the record and it plays through the side, twelve tracks in order; the synthesizer actually plays; every part moves.
    
    
    
      
      
    
      
    
        
    
          Side A · the one I write about
          
    Pink Floyd
    
          London · formed 1965
          
    Their argument, made quietly across two decades: institutions do not merely regulate us from the outside — they dictate our identity until we no longer realise who we truly are. I trace it through three songs: the self formed (Another Brick in the Wall), the self spent (Time) and the self lost (Wish You Were Here).
    
          
      
          
      Substack ↗Spotify ↗
      
          
      
            Read the essay — on Pink Floyd, alienation, and the self we lose · about 1,100 words
            
      
              
      Few bands have left a legacy as timeless as Pink Floyd’s. Their influence is immortalized through their visual symbols: a prism refracting white light into a spectrum of color, a burning handshake between two businessmen and a colossal wall built brick by brick across a stadium stage. These have become the defining symbols of the rock industry’s most ambitious band. However, these symbols conceal an underlying truth: over the course of two decades, the band’s main argument was that institutions do not merely regulate us from the outside. They dictate our identity until we no longer realize who we truly are.
      
              
      This argument is easy to miss because the band never tried to deliver it as a manifesto. Instead, it unfolds quietly across their catalogue, embedded within songs that explore ordinary forms of suffering: the vexation of school, the spectre of growing old, and the grief of bereavement. Considered individually, these songs read as disconnected accounts of personal loss. Collectively, however, they form a profound critique of modern society, with alienation at its very core.
      
              
      Alienation is the estrangement of a person from their own humanity within the very system they live in. This concept stems from Karl Marx, who argues that industrial capitalism alienates people from what they make, the work they do, and eventually the people around them. Pink Floyd’s ingenuity stemmed from their ability to reveal an abstract condition through the ordinary; a classroom, a wristwatch, the memory of a friend. This is traced across three stages: the self formed, the self spent, and the self lost.
      
              
      The first stage of alienation begins long before adulthood. Another Brick in the Wall, Pt. 2 (Pink Floyd’s iconic 1979 hit) presents the classroom not as a site of discovery, but as a factory. Students are manufactured rather than educated; their voices are suppressed into a single anonymous chorus until their individuality is gradually stripped away. This creates a homogeneous environment where individuals who conform to rules are rewarded and personal identity is diminished. The brick metaphor connotes that students are interchangeable components that are part of a larger system, shaped to fulfil institutional and societal expectations, rather than develop an identity of their own. Therefore, in this case, education becomes the first stage towards alienation. Half a century on, the wall has only risen higher. The schooling system has conditioned children to deem their worth through standardized tests, league tables, a resume, and the perfect career. This has resulted in a paradox where society cannot differentiate between education and the credentials associated with it. This is exactly what Pink Floyd warned us about five decades ago — modern society is alienated to the point where we are shaped into what the system will need long before we discover what we could have been.
      
              
      The child molded by their schooling journey eventually enters the workforce — the one they were trained for over twelve rigorous years. This sense of alienation does not disappear but instead becomes even more pronounced. If school forms our identity, Time shows us the progression of our work, our individuality over time. The song explores how modern society has become alienated to the point where we treat time as a commodity. The song opens with the overpowering noise of clocks ticking, emphasizing how each moment is fleeting in nature and reinforcing the passage of time. As years pass, our routines grow monotonous, and gradually we end up being consumed by the demands of everyday life. Time is no longer lived, but measured, and whatever can be measured can be owned, priced, and sold. Thus, life becomes a ledger where we measure ourselves through productivity; where productive hours are measured through output produced. The most disturbing aspect is that no bureaucratic authority is required to govern this system. There has been an internalization of discipline to the extent that we push ourselves beyond our limits to work harder, often mistaking constant productivity for ambition. This is the clearest form of alienation, as we no longer need someone to control us because we have learned to control ourselves. The song’s message resonates more with us, as that clock sits on our phones and wrists.
      
              
      This leaves the penultimate stage of estrangement and alienation: alienation from the self. Wish You Were Here, Pink Floyd’s elegy to Syd Barrett, exemplifies this through the depiction of a fractured sense of identity. The distinction between the innate self and the socially constructed self begins to collapse. Barrett functions as a symbol of the alienated subject that is consumed by the music industry, commodifying his creativity until he became inseparable from the product. The song’s continued longing illustrates saudade due to the unconscious desire to recover what has been lost, yet its absence remains unresolved. However, the song’s deeper yearning stems from missing a person who is not another human, but the innate self that we gradually lose over time. This thereby creates a divided self, where the identity that is presented to the world becomes detached from the self experienced internally. The bizarre emerges when our identity, something that should be most familiar to us, becomes strangely alien to us. In today’s modern age, this message resonates as we curate and tailor our identity to the audience and gradually begin to mistake the performance for the person. Thus, the more we conform to these societal expectations, the more estranged we are from our true self. Barrett was swallowed whole by an industry which metamorphosed a person into a product; whereas today we internalize the exact process through packaging the innumerable facades of our true self via our work, appearance, and status.
      
              
      Running in tandem, these songs function as anthems for the self formed, consumed, and estranged. Pink Floyd’s implications run far beyond the 1970s; what began as a warning from their own era has become the defining circumstance of ours. The monotonous machinery of alienation has further evolved with our generation in a more persuasive and internalized form than ever before. It begins with the classroom and then slowly creeps up at our workplace, and eventually it shapes inwards until we lose who we are. Moreover, what makes this progression more daunting, haunting, and disorienting is that it no longer requires an external force to act upon us; we carry it within us. The institutions that once controlled us have become the standards by which we judge our worthiness and control ourselves.
      
              
      Ultimately, it can be concluded with absolute certainty that no band or art will ever wield the same influence as Pink Floyd. They did more than just write about alienation; they gave it a medium, an image, and a voice to be represented through. Consequently, their legacy is epitomized through their warnings that have transcended decades to become reflections of our own.
